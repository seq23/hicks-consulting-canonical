How to Choose a Therapist Near You: A Practical Checklist

Author: Monika Hicks, LCSW · Read bio · August 27, 2026

Work through it in this order: confirm the therapist is licensed in the state where you will physically be during sessions, check that they treat what you are actually coming in for, understand what the credential letters after their name mean, settle the money questions before the first appointment, and use the consult call to test fit rather than to start the work.

Searching "therapist near me" returns results ranked by proximity, but the thing that actually determines whether someone can treat you is their state licence and their clinical fit, neither of which is a distance. With virtual care, the nearest therapist and the right therapist are often not the same person, and geography has quietly stopped being the first filter.

The checklist

  1. Decide where you will physically be during sessions. Licensing follows your location at the time of the appointment. This is the filter that eliminates the most options fastest, so do it first.
  2. Name what you are coming in for. Burnout, anxiety, a specific relationship, a life transition, grief, identity, or "I do not know, but something is off" — all are legitimate answers, and the last one is more common than people admit.
  3. Decide whether you need therapy or something else. Clinical therapy, non-clinical coaching, group support and organizational training solve different problems. Getting this wrong wastes months.
  4. Understand the credentials. See the table below. The letters matter less than people fear, but they do tell you what the person is trained and permitted to do.
  5. Sort out the money before the first session. Ask whether the practice bills insurance or is private pay, what a session costs, whether a superbill is available, and whether HSA or FSA payment is accepted.
  6. Ask about the cancellation policy. It is boring and it is the thing most likely to produce an unexpected charge. Most practices require 24 hours; some charge a set fee for less.
  7. Use the consult call as a consult call. It is for testing fit, not for starting the work. Ask how they typically work, what a first month looks like, and what they do when something is outside their scope.
  8. Check what they do not do. Many practices, including this one, do not provide crisis or emergency services. Know that before you need it, not during.
  9. Give it a few sessions, then re-check fit. Feeling slightly uncomfortable is often the work. Feeling unseen is usually a fit problem, and it is allowed to end.

What the letters generally mean

Titles and scope vary by state, so treat this as orientation rather than a rule, and confirm anything that matters with the licensing board in your own state.

Questions worth asking on the first call

  • Are you licensed in the state where I will be during our sessions?
  • Do you regularly work with what I am bringing in?
  • Do you bill insurance, or are you private pay? Is a superbill available?
  • What does a session cost, and how long is it?
  • What is your cancellation policy and the fee attached to it?
  • How often would you expect us to meet at the start?
  • What is outside your scope, and what do you do when something falls outside it?

Wanting a specific kind of therapist is a reasonable filter

People search for a Black therapist, a female therapist, or a therapist who can hold faith as part of the work, and those searches are not preferences to apologise for. Being able to skip the explaining is not a luxury; for many people it is the difference between doing the work and managing the room. Hicks Consulting primarily serves Black women and professionals navigating high-functioning stress, identity, life transitions and generational patterns, while serving clients of all ethnicities. If faith or spirituality is important to you, it can be thoughtfully integrated into the work rather than left at the door.

What this page recommends

Do steps one through three before you open a directory. Location, presenting concern, and therapy-versus-coaching narrow the field more than any amount of profile-reading, and they are the three questions a search engine cannot answer for you. Then book two consult calls rather than one. Comparing two conversations tells you more about fit than either conversation does alone.

Frequently asked questions

Does the therapist have to be near me?

For virtual care, no. What matters is that they are licensed in the state where you are physically located during sessions. Proximity stopped being the first filter once teletherapy became common.

What is the difference between a therapist and a psychologist?

Both may provide psychotherapy. Psychologists commonly hold a doctoral degree and in many cases provide formal psychological testing. Titles and scope vary by state, so confirm with your state licensing board.

Can a therapist prescribe medication?

Generally no. Prescribing is usually the role of a psychiatrist or another medical prescriber. A few states allow narrow prescribing authority for some psychologists.

How many sessions should I try before deciding it is not a fit?

There is no fixed number, but a few sessions is a reasonable window. Discomfort is often part of the work; feeling unseen is usually a fit problem, and changing therapists is allowed.
